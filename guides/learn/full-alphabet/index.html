Full alphabet

Fourth lesson. All 26 letters, in ordinary words.

Last updated: September 30, 2026

This is the first lesson that looks like normal reading. The row lessons each kept you inside one reach. Now the drill uses common English words, and any letter can follow any other. The words are easy: the, and, have, with, from, they, would, there. The challenge is moving between rows without losing your place, word after word.

What changes in this lesson

In the row lessons your hands could settle into one kind of movement. Here, “from” needs a home-row F, a top-row R and O, and a bottom-row M. The next word might start somewhere else entirely. The skill is returning to the F and J bumps between reaches so every word starts from a known position.

This is also where gaps from earlier lessons show up. If a letter you practised last week keeps going wrong here, that row probably got through on looking rather than on finger memory.

Technique

Type words, not letters

Common words such as “the”, “and” and “with” should become single movements. Your hands learn the shape of the whole word, much as you read a word without spelling it out. Aim to type each short word as one smooth burst, with a small pause at the space if you need it.

Keep an even rhythm

Try to give every keystroke roughly the same time. A fast burst on an easy word followed by a stall on a hard one causes more errors than a steady, slightly slower pace. Rhythm first, then speed.

Read one word ahead

While your fingers finish the current word, let your eyes move to the next one. This small look-ahead is what turns separate words into a continuous line. Don’t push it far. One word ahead is enough for now.

Let the space bar breathe

Many errors happen at word boundaries: the space comes too early, or the last letter is skipped. Finish the word, then press space with a deliberate tap.

The full-alphabet drill

The drill builds a line of about 120 characters from frequent English words. There is no backspace, so every miss stays marked. That is useful, because you can see exactly which letters caused trouble. WPM, accuracy, progress and errors update as you type. The lesson unlocks after bottom row, and finishing it unlocks numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Transposed letters. “teh” for “the” and “adn” for “and” happen when one hand runs ahead of the other. Slow down on those words until the order is reliable.
  • Doubled or missed spaces. Rushing the space bar either skips it or hits it twice. Treat space as a key that needs aiming, like any other.
  • Blaming the word. If the same letter goes wrong in many different words, the problem is that finger’s reach, not the vocabulary. Go back to the row lesson for that letter.
  • Chasing WPM too early. The first real-word lesson feels like it should be fast. Speed built on 85 percent accuracy doesn’t last. Keep accuracy near 95 percent.

Practice drills

  1. Top words (3 minutes). the of and to in is it you that he was for on are with as. These are among the most common words in English. Type the line until each word feels like one movement.
  2. Pangrams (3 minutes). the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog and pack my box with five dozen liquor jugs. Each uses every letter. Note which letters make you hesitate.
  3. Weak-letter list (2 minutes). Write down the two letters you missed most in the drill and type five words containing each. For example, for V and Y: very, give, over, move, live and you, year, yes, day, any.
  4. Steady sentences (2 minutes). Pick a sentence from any book and type it three times at the same, slower pace. Count the errors, not the time.

Moving to timed tests

Once this lesson feels steady, start taking a short timed test a few times a week. The practice guide explains the Timed, Words and Code modes. A 30-second test on Easy is a good start, and the goal is to stay near 95 percent accuracy before you worry about the WPM number. How to improve typing speed has a longer plan.

How to know you’re ready to move on

  • Common short words come out as single movements, without letter-by-letter thinking.
  • Accuracy on the drill stays around 95 percent.
  • Your hands return to home between words without you checking.
